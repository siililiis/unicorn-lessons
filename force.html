Force

Discover how force, distance, and a few clever tools let you lift, pull, and move far more than you'd think.

physics mechanics engineering

Lesson Overview

This lesson explores the connection between force and motion, by testing different ways the same result can be achieved with less muscular effort. Through hands-on tasks, students learn about simple mechanisms — the lever and the pulley system — and also learn how to tie a knot. Through hands-on testing, students see how the place, direction, and size of an applied force affects motion, and how mechanical tools make using force more efficient.

This lesson has four tasks:

  1. Square Knot
  2. Lever
  3. Lever Balance
  4. Pulley System
NB! Tasks 2 (Lever) and 3 (Lever Balance) teach the same principle. You can choose whichever seems like the better fit for your group, or do both if you'd like.

Theory: What makes things move?

Nothing moves on its own — that takes force. Force is like an invisible "push" or "pull" of energy that makes things move, or stops them. When you pull a sled, kick a football, or push a door shut, you're using force.

Force can do different things: it can make an object start moving, or stop something that's already moving; it can speed up or slow down motion; or it can change the direction of motion. Imagine riding a bike — when you brake, you use force to stop; when you pedal, you add force to go faster; and when you turn the handlebars, you use force to change direction.

There are many different kinds of force: muscular force (when you lift or move something yourself), wind force (a sailboat moves forward thanks to the wind), the force of flowing water (a watermill wheel turns), Earth's gravitational pull, or gravity (why things fall down), and magnetic force (an invisible force that pulls on metal). All of these forces can be used cleverly — and sometimes combined — to do big things with much less effort.

Good to know

  • Force is measured in Newtons (N), named after the famous scientist Isaac Newton, who studied gravity and the laws of motion.
  • Some animals can apply incredibly large forces relative to their own body weight — an ant, for example, can lift an object about 20 times heavier than itself.
  • Even light has force! It's called radiation pressure, and it's actually used in space to move solar sails.

Task 1: Square Knot

TASK 1

Tie a strong, easy-to-undo knot

Goal: learn to tie a knot that's strong, but still easy to undo afterwards.

Short description: students learn to tie the square knot, a classic favourite among sailors. It's used for joining two ends of equal thickness and holds firmly — but unlike a granny knot, it never jams, and stays easy to untie.

  1. Each student gets a piece of rope.
  2. Watch the video, or have the instructor demonstrate how to tie a square knot.
  3. Practise until everyone can tie it confidently.

Theory: the square knot has been used for centuries on sailing ships to tie down sails. It's also used to tie a triangular bandage for first aid, since the knot stays flat. Once tied, the knot is extremely strong, no matter how much force is applied to it. Unlike a granny knot — which, despite looking very similar, can afterwards be very hard, or sometimes even impossible, to untie — a square knot is wonderfully simple to undo again.

Task 2: Lever

TASK 2

Lift a weight you couldn't budge alone

Goal: use a lever to lift a weight that would be impossible to move without any tools.

Short description: the experiment uses a suitable lever — for example a strong wooden plank 1.5–2.5 metres long, a round pole, or a shovel handle (not included in the kit). The lever is rested on a fulcrum — a chair, a block of wood, or another stable object works well. Students then test how the lever lets them lift large weights.

  1. Find a suitable lever — for example a strong wooden plank 1.5–2.5 metres long, a round pole, or a shovel handle (not included in the kit).
  2. Rest the lever on a stable fulcrum, such as a chair, a block of wood, or another sturdy object.
  3. Place a weight on one end of the lever. You can use water-filled canisters, or the water-fillable bags included in the kit.
  4. Press down on the other end of the lever, and notice how much easier the weight is to lift.
  5. Experiment with moving the position of the weight and the fulcrum, to see how it affects the force needed to lift the weight.
Theory: the lever is one of the simplest and oldest mechanisms people have used for thousands of years to move heavy objects. It works by changing the length of the effort arm — the longer the effort arm, the less force is needed to lift the weight. Using a lever turns the force you apply into a larger output force, letting you lift or move objects that would be very difficult to shift with muscle power alone.

*Materials needed: a lever, a weight, and a chair or other object for the lever to rest on.

Good to know

  • The famous scientist Archimedes once said: "Give me a lever long enough and a fulcrum on which to place it, and I shall move the world."
  • Animals use levers in nature too — birds use their beak like a small lever to crack open seeds.

Task 3: Lever Balance

TASK 3

Balance a see-saw with load and effort

Goal: understand how the length of the effort arm affects balance and the amount of force needed.

Short description: students build a simple lever-balance system and test how changing the size and position of the weights affects reaching balance.

  1. Students work in pairs.
  2. A paperclip is attached to the table to act as the fulcrum. A wooden strip is used as the lever, with marbles in small plastic cups acting as weights.
  3. Place an equal number of marbles in each cup, and find the point where the lever balances.
  4. Change the position of one cup along the lever, and watch how the balance changes.
  5. Add more marbles to one cup, and test whether balance can be restored by moving the heavier cup closer to the fulcrum.
  6. Try some very unequal weights too, and look for a way to bring the lever back into balance.
Theory: the lever's working principle is simple: the longer the effort arm on the force side, the less force is needed to lift or balance the same weight. When a weight sits closer to the fulcrum, it effectively becomes "lighter." When a lever is balanced, the turning force (force × the length of the effort arm) is equal on both sides of the fulcrum.

We see levers in many everyday forms — a playground see-saw, scissors, pliers, a shovel, or even a bottle opener are all examples of a lever mechanism. Using a lever doesn't actually make a weight lighter — it lets you move it with less force, by spreading the work out over a longer distance of movement.

*Materials needed: a paperclip, a wooden strip, and marbles.

Good to know

  • People have used the lever principle for over 5,000 years — it's one of the oldest simple machines.
  • The famous scientist Archimedes once said: "Give me a lever long enough and a fulcrum on which to place it, and I shall move the world."
  • The human body has natural levers too — for example, your elbow joint acts like a lever whenever you bend or straighten your arm.

Task 4: Pulley System

TASK 4

Build a pulley system

Goal: build a pulley system, and understand how it reduces the force needed to lift a weight.

Short description: students build a pulley system using two triple pulley blocks and a rope, which lets them hoist heavy objects with much less effort. They test the system with different weights, and see how it reduces the force needed and makes lifting easier.

  1. Find a canister, or use the weighted bags included in the kit, and fill it with water.
  2. Find a suitable place to hang the pulley system — for example a hook, a beam, a staircase handrail, or a tree branch outside.
  3. Follow the video guide and connect two triple pulley blocks with rope to form a pulley system.
  4. Attach the pulley system to your chosen spot. You can use the rope pieces included in the kit, secured with the square knot you learned earlier.
  5. Test the pulley system with different weights.
  6. If conditions are safe, you can try lifting a classmate — or even yourself.
  7. When lowering the weight, hold the rope firmly and let it slide slowly through your palm, to avoid hurting your hands.
Theory: a pulley system is a simple mechanism that helps lift or move heavy objects using less force. It works because the weight is shared across several sections of rope, which makes it easier to lift.
A pulley system is made up of one or more pulleys (wheels) with a rope or cable running around them. Moving pulleys reduce the amount of force needed to lift the weight, while fixed pulleys change the direction of the force — for example, letting you pull downward to lift something upward.
The more wheels and rope sections a pulley system has, the less force is needed to lift the same weight — though this means you'll need to pull much more rope through to lift it the same distance.

*Materials needed: pulley blocks, carabiners, a long rope, and weights.

Good to know

  • Pulley systems have been indispensable on sailing ships for centuries, reducing the force needed to raise and move the sails.
  • Modern elevators combine the pulley principle with counterweights, making movement even smoother and more energy-efficient.
  • The more pulleys a system has, the less force is needed... but the longer the rope you'll need to pull through, to lift the weight the same height.

Tips for instructors

  • Let students practise the square knot on a spare piece of rope a few times before tying it "for real" — it makes the motion click faster.
  • For the lever tasks, let students discover it themselves — encourage them to move the fulcrum or the weight's position rather than telling them what will happen.
  • Do a dry run with a light weight on the pulley system before anyone attempts to lift a person.
  • Keep the safety point about lowering weights slowly front of mind — rope burns are a real risk with the pulley task.

Troubleshooting

  • Knot won't hold, or comes apart? Check that both loops go through the knot the same way — that's what makes it a square knot rather than a granny knot.
  • Lever doesn't seem to make lifting any easier? Move the fulcrum closer to the weight, or use a longer lever.
  • Lever won't balance? Double-check that both cups start with an equal number of marbles, and that the fulcrum is exactly centred before adjusting anything else.
  • Pulley system feels just as hard to pull as lifting directly? Check the rope is threaded properly through every wheel, and that nothing is snagging.

Career path: who works with force & mechanisms? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed lifting, balancing, and hoisting today, you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Mechanical Engineers — design machines and mechanisms that turn force into useful motion.
  • Civil Engineers — use levers, pulleys, and force calculations to design safe bridges and buildings.
  • Physicists — study the fundamental laws of force and motion.
  • Sailors & Riggers — rely on knots and pulley systems every day to control sails and equipment.
  • Crane Operators — use pulley and lever principles at a massive scale to lift heavy loads on construction sites.
