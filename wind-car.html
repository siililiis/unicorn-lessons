Wind Car

Catch the wind on dry land — build a sail-powered car, race it down a three-metre track, then work out how to keep moving when the wind is against you.

physics wind energy engineering

Lesson Overview

In this lesson students investigate how wind energy can be used to travel on land. Working in pairs, students build a small wind car, test whether it moves when the "wind" is switched on, and explore how the vehicle behaves as the wind direction changes.

Goal: build a vehicle that rolls on wheels and is driven forward by wind. Test how the size, shape and position of the sail affect how the car moves in a tailwind, a crosswind and a headwind.

This lesson has one task:

  1. Wind car in a tailwind

And a bonus task if there is time left over: Wind car in a crosswind or headwind.

Theory: How does a wind car work?

A wind car is a land vehicle that works on exactly the same principle as a sailing boat at sea — the sail catches the energy of the wind and turns it into a force that pushes the vehicle forward.

Moving is easiest when the wind blows from behind. The air pushes straight into the sail and the car rolls along without much effort. If the wind comes from the side, the sail has to be turned to the right angle so the car still moves forward. Sailing straight into the wind is impossible — but you can still make progress by tacking, that is, zig-zagging so that the wind hits first one side of the sail and then the other. Sailing boats at sea use exactly the same trick to reach a destination that lies directly upwind.

Balance between the sail and the car matters. If the body of the car is too small and the sail too large, the wind will simply blow the whole machine over. If the sail is too heavy or the wrong shape, it won't catch enough wind and the car won't move at all. So the job is to test which combination works best — and that is exactly what engineering is, and where the joy of discovery comes from. :)

Good to know

  • Wind cars aren't just toys — they're used in real sport. There are racing competitions where land yachts tear along beaches and salt flats at over 100 km/h.
  • Some record-breaking land yachts have reached speeds of over 200 km/h using nothing but the power of the wind!
  • A wind car works on the same principle as a sailing boat — the only difference is that wheels replace the floating hull, and the vehicle travels over land.

Task 1: Wind Car in a Tailwind

TASK 1

Build a wind car and send it down the track

Goal: build a simple wind car and test how wind energy sets it moving in a tailwind. Students experience first-hand how the size and shape of the sail affect the speed and stability of the vehicle.

Task description: students use the wheels and axles from the kit (or build wheels and axles of their own from, say, cardboard or bottle caps and barbecue skewers) along with other materials to build a small vehicle with a sail. Fans create the wind, and the class watches how the car travels from point A to point B. If they like, pairs can add a Lego passenger to the car, who has to stay aboard all the way to the finish.

Activity guide

  1. Split the students into pairs. Each pair builds one vehicle together.
  2. Give each pair wheels, axles and other building materials (paper, cardboard, drinking straws and so on), or set up one large shared building area.
  3. NB! In the video tutorial the demonstration car is built with CD wheels. Your kit contains bottle caps and plastic wheels, plus metal axles. If students build with the metal axles and plastic wheels, those have to go back into the box after the lesson. If they make the wheels from bottle caps instead, they can keep the finished vehicle.
  4. Introduce the challenge: build a vehicle that can travel at least 3 m using wind energy (created by the fans) as its driving force.
  5. Make space on the floor. Lay a strip of tape on the floor as the starting line and measure 3 m from there to the finish (you can also start from a shorter distance, e.g. 2 m). Place three fans at the starting line to create a tailwind. Connect the fans to the mains using an extension cord. Check that the "wind" is blowing.
    Test track — tailwind setup
  6. Let the cars go one at a time and see whether they make it across the finish line.
  7. If you like, measure the longest run as well.
Theory: a wind car works on the same principle as a sailing boat — the sail catches the wind coming from behind and pushes the vehicle forward. The bigger the sail, the more air it catches and the greater the driving force. But if the sail is too big or the car too light, the wind will blow the car over — so the trick is finding the balance between sail and car.
Materials needed: wind car building materials (in the box: wheels, axles; plus paper, pieces of cardboard, drinking straws, tape, scissors and so on), fans.

Examples of different solutions:

NB!

Let the children try and experiment for themselves before you hand out any hints! Don't show them the solutions straight away. If a pair hasn't come up with a single idea within 10 minutes, start giving hints or help them along a little.

Hints if a pair gets stuck

  • What other vehicles move using wind power? Could you borrow an idea from one of them?
  • The bigger the sail, the more wind it catches.
  • But if the sail is far too big for the car, the wind will simply blow the car over.

Remember: hints come only after the pair has had a proper go on its own — roughly 10 minutes of trying first.

Troubleshooting: why won't the car move?

  • The axle is fixed straight onto the base of the car. Wheels can only roll if the axle runs through a straw that is attached to the frame.
  • The wheels are rubbing against the frame or the straw.
  • The sail sits too high and the air passes underneath it.
  • The sail is the wrong shape and doesn't catch the wind.
  • The car and the sail are out of proportion with each other.

Bonus Task: Wind Car in a Crosswind or Headwind

Only if there is time left over.

BONUS

Sail across the wind — and against it

Goal: test how a wind car moves in a crosswind or a headwind. Students discover that sailing straight into the wind is impossible, but that by changing the position of the sail and using a zig-zag route you can still make progress.

Task description: each pair takes the wind car they built in Task 1 and tries changing the position of its sail, or builds a new sail, so that the vehicle can travel across the wind or against it. For a crosswind it's usually enough to change the angle of the sail; for a headwind the car has to travel at a sharp angle to the wind and "zig-zag".

Activity guide

  1. Position the fans so that the wind blows from the side, or straight towards the car.
  2. Test how the car moves in a crosswind: turn the corner of the sail and fix it at a suitable angle. Watch whether the car moves, and in which direction.
  3. Test whether the car can also move against the wind: try the "zig-zag", or tacking, method — steer the car first along one side of the wind direction and then along the other.
Tacking: the zig-zag route into the wind
Theory: when a wind car travels in a tailwind, the motion is fairly simple — the wind blows straight into the sail and pushes the car forward. When the wind comes from the side, or straight on, things get more complicated. In a crosswind it's still possible to move the car if the sail is set at a suitable angle (roughly 45°–60° to the wind direction).
If the sail is completely side-on to the wind, the air just slides across it and the car goes nowhere. But set the sail at a gentle angle and the wind "catches" on it — part of the force pushes the car sideways, and part of it pushes the car forward. That's how a wind car can move even when the wind blows from the side, and it's exactly how sailing boats at sea travel when they don't have a straight tailwind.
Sailing straight into the wind is not possible, because the wind presses against the sail and simply pushes the car back. You can, however, travel at a sharp angle to the wind direction. In sailing this technique is called tacking — the sail is turned so that the wind pushes the vehicle first from one side and then from the other, and the craft still makes progress along a zig-zag path. This is how land yachts and boats cover distance even when their destination lies directly into the wind.

Reflection

Keep it spoken and group-based — a quick conversation, not a worksheet.

1. What went well for you today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What was the trickiest part, and how did you work through it?
  • What are you most proud of?
  • When did you realise "now it's working"?

2. What did you learn today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What can you do now that you couldn't this morning?
  • If you had to teach one thing from today to a friend or sibling, what would it be?
  • Why does that matter — what is it useful for?

3. Where else could you use this?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • Does any school subject use similar thinking (e.g. maths, art, PE)? Which one, and how?
  • Have you used something like this before — at home or in a game?
  • Who else uses this in real life — what job might that be? (See the Career path section below for ideas.)
Notes for instructors: keep the discussion spoken and group-based rather than written or individual. The follow-up prompts are a fallback, not a script — if the group answers well on the first try, move on rather than working through the whole list.

Career path: who works with wind? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed turning moving air into motion today, you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Wind Energy Engineers — design the turbines and wind farms that turn wind into electricity.
  • Aerodynamicists — study how air flows around cars, aircraft and buildings, and shape them to work with it.
  • Yacht & Sail Designers — design hulls and sails that get the most out of every gust.
  • Meteorologists — measure and forecast wind and weather for shipping, aviation and energy companies.
  • Mechanical Engineers — build the wheels, axles and moving parts that let a machine roll smoothly.
