Lesson Overview

This lesson introduces stencil art and layered spray painting. Students try out graffiti technique, practise precision and cutting skills, and learn the principles of layering and contrast.

This lesson has one task:

  1. Stencil Graffiti
There's also a bonus task below — Your Own Portrait Stencil — for groups with extra time, interest, and access to a printer.

Theory: What is stencil art?

Stencil art is a technique where an image is created using a stencil — usually a shape cut out of paper, cardboard, or another thin material, through which paint is applied to a surface. Once the stencil is removed, what's left behind is a sharp, contrasting shape made up of painted and unpainted areas.

This technique makes it easy to quickly repeat the same image in different places and colours. Using several layers and different colours adds depth and detail to a piece, creating a more three-dimensional impression. It matters which parts of the image sit in the foreground and which sit in the background, since every layer of colour affects the final result.

Stencil technique is widely used both in street art (think of Banksy's work) and in stage design and advertising, since it's fast, precise, and creates a strong visual effect.

Good to know

  • If graffiti is made without the property owner's permission, it counts as vandalism.
  • Early forms of graffiti already existed in Ancient Rome, where people carved pictures and messages into buildings.
  • Modern graffiti, as we know it today, began in the late 1960s in Philadelphia, USA.
  • Stencil art is used in all kinds of fields — from street warning signs all the way to professional fine art.
  • With stencils and layered spray painting, it's possible to create very detailed, large-scale artwork.
  • Curious what it looks like in action? Search YouTube for "stencil graffiti," "stencil graffiti art," or "layered stencil" — or watch the example video linked below.

Task 1: Stencil Graffiti

TASK 1

Build a giant stencil graffiti — together

Watch the video tutorial

Goal: work together to create one large stencil-technique graffiti piece, practising cutting a stencil, working in layers, and using spray paint to achieve a bold, detailed result.

Short description: as a group, students build one large stencil graffiti. The stencil used in this lesson is made up of 8 A4 pieces (plus 1 separate face piece), which are later fitted together like a puzzle into one picture. If there are more students, pieces can be cut in half again so everyone gets a piece to cut out. Only the dark/grey areas are cut away from the paper. The finished pieces are then put up together on a wall, a larger sheet of paper, or a canvas, held in place with tape or reusable adhesive. Once the picture is assembled, the whole stencil is sprayed with an even layer of paint. The face piece is sprayed separately, in a different colour, as its own layer.

  1. Before cutting, put the picture together as a trial run — lay out all the pieces in the right order so it's clear where each part belongs. If you like, number the pieces on the back so they're easier to put back in the right place later.
  2. Divide the picture pieces among the students — each student gets one stencil piece (8 in total, plus 1 separate face piece). If there are more students, cut the pieces in half so everyone gets a share.
  3. Trace over the outlines you're about to cut with a marker.
  4. Cut out the dark/grey areas, following the outline.
  5. Attach the first layer — the whole face piece — to the wall, large paper, or canvas, and spray it with a contrasting colour (not black). Let it dry for a moment.
  6. Assemble the whole picture: attach all the pieces to the same wall, paper, or canvas using tape or reusable adhesive. Make sure the pieces form a correct, complete picture. Spray the second layer.
  7. Let it dry a little, then remove the stencils.

*Materials needed: stencil picture pieces, cutting mats, craft knives/scalpels, tape/reusable adhesive, markers, spray paint, a canvas/larger sheet of paper/free wall space.

Cutting & layering tips

  • If a cut goes wrong, it's no big deal — anything can be fixed with tape! :)
  • When lining up the face layer with the rest of the picture, make sure they don't sit exactly on top of each other. You can also agree in advance which parts of the face will be cut out for the contrast colour, so they don't fully overlap with the next colour layer's cutouts. This adds more depth and detail to the picture.
  • You don't need to cut out every tiny grey/dark area with perfect precision. If an area is too fiddly or detailed, cut it as a bigger, simpler shape instead. That said, the more precise the cut, the better the final result.

Spray paint safety

  • The paints used are water-based and currently the most kid-friendly available. Even so, it's best to spray outdoors or in a room with open windows.
  • Before spraying, shake the can firmly up and down for at least a minute, then hold it about 20 cm from the surface and spray quickly across it in a thin layer. Staying too long in one spot will make the paint run and stop it drying properly.

After using a spray can

Hold the can upright and give it a quick shake.
Turn the can upside down (the tube inside no longer reaches the paint).
Press the nozzle for 1–3 seconds — only gas should come out, not paint.
Put the cap back on and return the can to the box, upright.
Rinse the spray nozzles briefly in water and clean them before putting them back in the box — this stops paint drying inside the nozzle, so the next group can use the same one.

Bonus Task: Your Own Portrait Stencil Optional — needs a printer

BONUS

Turn your own portrait into stencil art

Goal: turn a portrait photo of yourself into a stencil-technique picture.

Short description: students take a portrait photo of themselves, process it into a black-and-white image suited to stencil technique, and cut the black areas out as a stencil. The image is then sprayed onto paper or canvas, creating a high-contrast portrait.

  1. Take a portrait photo of yourself against a white or light-coloured background.
  2. Upload the photo to a stencil-making tool (e.g. Rapid Resizer).
  3. Adjust the Threshold and Dark-White sliders so the picture is left with clear, not overly detailed black areas. If needed, adjust the Sharp-Soft slider for a softer result.

The stencil-making tool:

  1. Download the processed image and print it (ideally on heavier paper, around 160g).
  2. Using a craft knife or scalpel, cut out only the black areas.
  3. Place the finished stencil on another sheet of A4 paper or a canvas.
  4. Spray paint over the whole stencil from about 20 cm away, in a thin layer.
  5. Carefully lift the stencil away and let the picture dry.

*Materials needed: access to a printer, heavier paper (~160g), a craft knife or scalpel, A4 paper or canvas, spray paint.

Important

If you cut the wrong spot by accident, it can always be fixed with tape.

You don't need to cut out every tiny black dot with perfect precision — if an area is too detailed or "fiddly," cut a bigger, simpler shape instead. That said, the more precisely you cut, the more recognisable the final result will be. :)

Work with spray paint outdoors, in a well-ventilated room, or by an open window.

Spray from about 20 cm away. Don't let the paint linger too long in one spot — it will start to run and won't dry properly.

Several thin layers work better than one thick one.

Cover the table or floor with paper or plastic sheeting to avoid getting paint on other surfaces.

Tips for instructors

Before spraying

  • Do the trial "puzzle" assembly early — laying out all the pieces before anyone starts cutting avoids confusion about which piece is whose later on.
  • Number the backs of the pieces if the group is large or the picture is complex — it saves time when reassembling on the wall.
  • Set up outdoors or by an open window before spraying begins, so there's no scramble once paint is already out.

Troubleshooting

  • Cut a piece wrong? Tape it back together from behind — it's barely noticeable once sprayed.
  • Paint is running or dripping? The can was likely held too close, or too long in one spot — move faster and keep about 20 cm of distance.
  • Spray nozzle stops spraying? It's probably clogged with dried paint — check that nozzles were rinsed after their last use.
  • Layers look too flat, with no depth? Encourage students to offset the face layer slightly from the rest of the picture, rather than lining it up exactly.

Reflection

Keep it spoken and group-based — a quick conversation, not a worksheet.

1. What went well for you today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What was the trickiest part, and how did you work through it?
  • What are you most proud of?
  • When did you realise "now it's working"?

2. What did you learn today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What can you do now that you couldn't this morning?
  • If you had to teach one thing from today to a friend or sibling, what would it be?
  • Why does that matter — what is it useful for?

3. Where else could you use this?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • Does any school subject use similar thinking (e.g. maths, art, PE)? Which one, and how?
  • Have you used something like this before — at home or in a game?
  • Who else uses this in real life — what job might that be? (See the Career path section below for ideas.)
Notes for instructors: keep the discussion spoken and group-based rather than written or individual. The follow-up prompts are a fallback, not a script — if the group answers well on the first try, move on rather than working through the whole list.

Career path: who works with stencils & design? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed cutting, layering, and spraying today, you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Graphic Designers — use layers, contrast, and shape to design everything from posters to logos.
  • Street Artists & Muralists — use stencils and spray paint to create large-scale public artwork.
  • Illustrators — turn images and ideas into striking visual designs.
  • Set & Stage Designers — use stencil techniques to create backdrops and props quickly and precisely.
  • Advertising & Brand Designers — use bold, high-contrast visuals to make a design instantly recognisable.
