Fan

Air is a gas you can push around — solder together a motor-driven fan, then send a balloon rocket racing down a line.

physics electronics engineering

Lesson Overview

Air is a gas. There are all sorts of ways to force air or gas to move: you can fan yourself with a hand fan, or direct it with an air blower, as in another of our lessons. Today we try building a motorised solution — a fan. A fan is a machine with blades or vanes that creates a forced flow of air. Fans come in many sizes and shapes, and are most often used to cool something down quickly. You've probably noticed yourself that on a hot summer day it's rather nice to sit in front of one.

The second task is a simple demonstration of how air trapped inside a balloon is pushed out of the open end, which makes the balloon shoot forward. A real rocket engine works on exactly the same principle.

The aims of this lesson are to:

  1. build a motorised fan, practising soldering along the way,
  2. make a balloon rocket.

The lesson has two parts: in the first, students build a small fan that runs on a motor and batteries; in the second, they make a balloon rocket that runs along a washing line, using a balloon, a drinking straw and tape.

Theory: pushing air on purpose

Air isn't "nothing" — it's a mixture of gases, and although we can't see it, it has mass and takes up space. It's made of countless tiny particles that are constantly moving in every direction. Whenever you get air particles moving together in one direction, you feel it as wind, and it can push on things.

A fan does this deliberately. A motor spins a set of angled blades, and each blade scoops air and throws it backwards — so a steady stream of air comes out of one side while more air is drawn in at the other. Which way the blades spin decides which way the air goes, and that in turn depends on which way round the current flows through the motor. Swap the two wires and the same fan sucks instead of blows.

A balloon rocket gets its movement from the same idea, without a motor. The stretched rubber squeezes the air inside the balloon, and when you let go, that air rushes out of the neck in one direction — and the balloon shoots off in the opposite one. Every push in one direction comes with an equal push the other way. That is exactly how a real rocket engine works: it doesn't push against the air or the ground, it simply throws gas backwards very fast.

Task 1: Build Your Own Fan

TASK 1

A fan from a motor, a propeller and two batteries

Goal: build a working fan out of a small motor, a plastic propeller, batteries and a wooden stick — and practise soldering while doing it.

Students build the fan either in pairs or individually. To build one fan you need a wooden stick, a small DC motor, a motor mount (a plastic pipe clamp), a screw, a battery holder, a propeller and a little insulating tape.

Watch the video tutorial
Parts for one fan

Tools on top of this: a cordless drill (for the screw) and a soldering iron.

⚠️ NB! Soldering safety

A soldering iron tip reaches several hundred degrees. Set up the soldering stations in one fixed place with their stands, agree before you start that the iron always goes back into its stand and that only one pair solders at a time, and keep hair tied back and sleeves clear. Air the room, and remind everyone that the tip stays hot for a good while after it has been switched off.

Activity guide

  1. Find all the parts you need.
  2. Attach the motor mount to the wooden stick. Place the motor mount on the end of the stick and fix it with the screw. Use the cordless drill to drive the screw in.
  3. Check which way round the motor has to spin. Put the propeller on the motor shaft and the batteries in the battery holder. Connect one of the battery holder's leads to one motor terminal and the other lead to the other terminal, but don't solder them yet. Switch the battery holder on and check whether the fan blows air forwards or pulls air towards itself. If it pulls, swap the two wires over on the motor terminals and try again. If it helps, mark the wire end and its matching terminal so you know which way round to solder them.
    Check before you solder
  4. Solder the wires to the motor terminals. If the bare metal at the end of the battery holder's leads is too short, strip a little more insulation off first. Then solder both wires to the correct motor terminals.
  5. Fit the motor into the mount. Place the motor into the plastic mount on the stick.
  6. Attach the battery holder to the wooden stick. Place the battery holder against the lower part of the stick and wrap insulating tape around both the holder and the stick, so that the holder is firmly attached.
  7. The fan is ready! Switch it on and check that everything works properly.
    How it all goes together
  8. Balloon transport. Try to keep a balloon up in the air with the fans and move it from point A to point B. Hint: one fan probably won't manage it — you'll need several fans, which means teamwork!
  9. Table tennis ball accuracy test. Place a plastic cup on the table (or on the floor) and try to blow a table tennis ball into the cup with your fan.
Think up more fun games of your own! :)

After the lesson: take the fans apart

The fans have to be dismantled again at the end of the lesson so the parts go back into the box:

  • Desolder the wires from the motor — melt the solder again and carefully pull the wire away.
  • Remove the insulating tape holding the battery holder to the stick.
  • Drive the screws back out of the wooden sticks with the drill. The drill has a button on its side that sets the direction of rotation — use it to put the drill into reverse.

Task 2: Balloon Rocket

TASK 2

Build a rocket that travels on air power

Task: build a rocket that can move forward using nothing but the force of escaping air.

Watch the video tutorial
Have a look at the info sheet as well. There's a laminated copy of it in the box: Balloon rocket info sheet — open it if you want to show it on a big screen or print extra copies.

Setting up

Tie one end of the string to a chair, a table, a door handle or similar, and pull it taut. Don't tie the second end down straight away — first thread on the drinking straw that will run back and forth along the line. If there's nowhere to fasten the second end, someone can simply hold it. You can set up several tracks, and they can be different lengths (3 m, 5 m, or even 10 m).

Balloon rocket track

Activity guide

  1. Students work in teams of 2–4.
  2. To solve the task you need a balloon, a drinking straw and tape (a clothes peg is optional). Instead of a straw you can also thread a couple of key rings onto the line — they run along it just as freely.
  3. The students have to work out how to get the balloon travelling along the string in the right direction. The goal is to get the balloon from one end of the string to the other.
  4. The task is solved in teams, helping each other pump the balloon up and fasten it to the line.
  5. Once a balloon has made its run, it has to be taken off the straw and the straw returned to the start so the next pair can test their rocket. The same balloon can be inflated again and again. Be careful not to pump too much air into it — otherwise the balloon simply bursts.
  6. After the task, watch this one-minute explainer video together with the students: youtu.be/dX_32EDz0jM

NB!

Once the tracks are set up, everyone has to move carefully between them — walk alongside a track, or go around it in a wide arc, rather than through it.

Very important!

After inflating a balloon, don't tie a knot in it. Just twist the neck and either clip it with a clothes peg or hold it closed with your fingers until the moment of release. Try to get the longest run you can!

Tips for instructors

  • Set up the soldering station and the rules first, before any building starts — irons in their stands, one pair soldering at a time, hair tied back.
  • Test the motor leads before soldering. A one-second touch shows which way the propeller spins; solder them that way round and nobody has to redo a joint.
  • Don't inflate the balloons to the very last puff — slightly less air means they survive to be used again.
  • Remind the group how to move around the rocket lines — taut string across a room is easy to walk into.
  • Set up the rocket tracks before the lesson if you can, or at least decide where they'll go, so the changeover between the two tasks is quick.

Troubleshooting

  • The fan sucks instead of blowing? Swap the two leads from the battery holder over on the motor terminals.
  • The motor doesn't turn at all? Check the solder joints are shiny and solid, that the batteries are the right way round in the holder, and that the wire ends are stripped back to bare metal.
  • The propeller wobbles or rubs? Push it all the way onto the shaft and check it's sitting straight.
  • The balloon rocket doesn't move? The string probably isn't taut enough, or the balloon is taped on crooked — its neck has to point straight back along the line.
  • The balloon comes off the straw? Use two pieces of tape, and make sure they stick to the straw itself rather than only to the balloon.

Reflection

Keep it spoken and group-based — a quick conversation, not a worksheet.

1. What went well for you today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What was the trickiest part, and how did you work through it?
  • What are you most proud of?
  • When did you realise "now it's working"?

2. What did you learn today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What can you do now that you couldn't this morning?
  • If you had to teach one thing from today to a friend or sibling, what would it be?
  • Why does that matter — what is it useful for?

3. Where else could you use this?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • Does any school subject use similar thinking (e.g. maths, art, PE)? Which one, and how?
  • Have you used something like this before — at home or in a game?
  • Who else uses this in real life — what job might that be? (See the Career path section below for ideas.)
Notes for instructors: keep the discussion spoken and group-based rather than written or individual. The follow-up prompts are a fallback, not a script — if the group answers well on the first try, move on rather than working through the whole list.

Career path: who works with moving air? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed soldering a motor together and pushing air around with it today, you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Electronics Technicians — solder and assemble the circuits inside almost every electrical thing you own.
  • Mechanical Engineers — design the fans, pumps and turbines that move air, water and gas.
  • Ventilation Engineers — make sure homes, schools and hospitals have fresh air moving through them.
  • Rocket Engineers — build engines that work exactly like the balloon: throw gas backwards, go forwards.
  • Aerodynamicists — study how air flows around objects and shape them to work with it.
