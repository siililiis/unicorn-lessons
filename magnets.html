Magnets

Discover the invisible forces all around us — find true north, then spin a motor with nothing but a battery and a magnet.

physics magnetism engineering

Lesson Overview

This lesson introduces students to magnets and magnetism through hands-on exploration. Students discover what magnetic poles are, learn that Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet, and then build simple motors that turn electricity and magnetism directly into motion.

This lesson has two tasks:

  1. Finding North
  2. Propeller Motor (with a Bonus alternative: World's Simplest Motor)

Theory: What is a magnet?

Objects that can attract light objects made of iron are called magnets. A magnet has two poles — a north pole and a south pole — and together, these two poles create a magnetic field around the magnet. Push a north pole and a south pole together, and they attract. Push together two like poles (two north poles, say), and they repel.

People first came across magnetism while examining pieces of iron ore. They noticed that small iron particles would "stick" to certain pieces of ore — and that's essentially how magnets were first discovered.

There are many kinds of magnets. Ordinary magnets — like the ones on your fridge — keep their magnetic properties all the time, or for a very long time. These are called permanent magnets. But objects that simply come into contact with a magnet can pick up magnetic properties too — for example, rubbing a screwdriver tip against a magnet will magnetise the screwdriver, letting it pick up small metal objects. Objects magnetised this way aren't permanent magnets, though — their magnetism can be removed again, for instance by striking them, dropping them, or heating them strongly.

At its core, a magnet is simply an object that creates a magnetic field around itself, and it's this field that lets magnets pull and push on each other from a distance. A magnetic field isn't visible, and you can't feel it directly — but you can reveal its shape with a simple experiment using iron filings, like in the photo at the top of this page.

Good to know

  • Fridge magnets are permanent magnets — they hold onto their magnetism for a very long time without needing anything to "recharge" them.
  • A magnetised screwdriver, on the other hand, isn't permanent — you can "erase" its magnetism by striking it, dropping it, or heating it strongly.
  • Because opposite poles attract, a freely spinning magnet always settles pointing north-south — which is exactly why a compass works.

Task 1: Finding North

TASK 1

Build a homemade compass

Goal: understand what Earth's magnetic field is and how a compass works, then use a homemade compass to find true north.

  1. Discuss as a group: what are magnets, and why do some ends attract while others repel? What different kinds of magnets exist?
  2. Explain what Earth's magnetic field is, and how a compass works. Watch this explainer video together: "What is a compass?"
  3. Try to determine north using a bowl, a needle, and a magnet. If the needle won't rest on the water's surface on its own, try resting it on the small piece of lightweight polystyrene included in the kit instead. Do the experiment individually or in pairs. NB! Keep all magnets well away from the bowls during this step.
  4. To remove the needle's magnetism afterwards, drop it a few times or tap it lightly.
Theory — Earth's magnetic field: our planet is like one giant magnet, because deep inside the Earth there's a huge amount of metal. Earth has a magnetic north and south pole too (north = N, south = S), and it's surrounded by a magnetic field. Because of this field, a compass's magnetised needle always turns so that its ends line up with Earth's magnetic poles.
By convention, we call Earth's "north pole" the pole that the north-seeking end of a compass needle points toward. But since opposite poles attract, strictly speaking, Earth's magnetic north pole is actually located near the geographic south pole, and vice versa! Even so, in everyday and geographic language, the pole a compass needle points toward is still simply called north.

Extra fact: wandering poles

Earth's magnetic poles swap places roughly every 300,000 years — though the last swap happened about 780,000 years ago, so it's nearly impossible to predict exactly when the next one will be. Right now, Earth's geographic north pole and its magnetic north pole are offset from each other by about 11 degrees, and the poles drift at roughly 40 km per year. So when using a magnet or compass to find north, keep in mind it's slightly offset from true geographic north — newer compasses already correct for this, so they still point you the right way. :)

Task 2: Propeller Motor

TASK 2

Build the world's simplest motor

Goal: each student builds her own tiny propeller that spins using nothing but a battery, a magnet, and a bit of wire.

Watch the video tutorial
  1. Each student gets one AA battery, 2 magnets, one screw, and about a 10 cm length of copper wire.
  2. First, make the propeller blades: cut 4 strips of coloured paper, each about 4 cm long and 1 cm wide.
  3. Glue the strips together at the centre so they form a propeller shape, like in the photo above.
  4. Place the propeller between the two magnets, then set the screw on top of the magnets.
  5. Cut a suitably long piece of copper wire (about 10 cm) and bend it slightly so that one end reaches the battery's "+" end and the other reaches the magnet.
  6. Bend the tip of the wire a little more — this makes it easier to hold it steady in place.
  7. Rest the screw's tip against the battery's "−" end. While holding the top of the wire against the battery with your finger, bring the wire's lower end close to the magnet.
The screw will start spinning, and your simple propeller motor is ready! :)

Bonus / Alternative Task: World's Simplest Motor

This task can be done instead of the Propeller Motor above.

BONUS

Build a homopolar motor

Watch the video tutorial

Goal: build a simple homopolar motor to understand how electric current and a magnetic field combine to create motion, and how electrical energy converts into mechanical energy.

Short description: a magnet is placed on the battery's negative end, and a piece of copper wire is bent so that its top end rests on the battery's positive terminal, while its bottom ends lightly touch (or hover just above) the magnet. Together, the electric current and the magnetic field make the wire spin.

  1. Each student builds her own simple motor.
  2. Place one or more magnets on the battery's negative end (the flat side).
  3. Bend the copper wire into a shape where its top end rests on the battery's positive terminal, and its bottom ends sit just above, or lightly touch, the magnet.
  4. Place the wire onto the battery so that it rests on the positive terminal and stays in contact with the magnet.
  5. If the connection is right, the wire will start spinning, driven by the combined effect of the magnetic field and the electric current.
  6. Try different wire shapes and see how they affect the spinning.
Theory: this motor's working principle is based on the Lorentz force, which occurs when electric current moves through a magnetic field. Because the magnetic field's polarity stays constant and the current always flows in one direction, this is called a "homo-" (same-polarity) motor. This simple device shows how electrical energy converts directly into mechanical motion.

*Materials needed: a magnet, a battery, copper wire.

NB!

If bending the wire into a heart shape feels too tricky or time-consuming, feel free to use a simpler shape instead. See more examples here: youtu.be/0DwHz0zRIGM

Tips for instructors

  • Demo the floating needle once before pairs try it themselves — the polystyrene trick works best when the needle is dry and freshly magnetised.
  • Encourage neat, even paper strips for the propeller — a well-balanced propeller spins much more smoothly.
  • Let students experiment freely with wire shapes on the homopolar motor — a slightly lopsided loop often spins just as well, and trying different shapes is part of the fun.
  • Keep spare batteries, magnets, and wire on hand — fumbling fingers occasionally bend a wire past the point of no return.

Troubleshooting

  • Needle won't stay magnetised? Stroke it repeatedly in the same direction with a strong magnet before floating it, and keep other magnets well away from the bowl during the test.
  • Propeller doesn't spin, or only spins for a moment? Check the wire is making steady contact with both the battery's "+" terminal and the magnet, and confirm the screw tip is properly touching the battery's "−" end.
  • Homopolar motor won't start? Double-check the wire is making a clean, bare-metal contact with both the battery terminal and the magnet — anything in between (tape, paint, dirt) can block the current.
  • Motor spins briefly, then stops? The wire has probably shifted out of contact — gently reposition it against the magnet.

Reflection

Keep it spoken and group-based — a quick conversation, not a worksheet.

1. What went well for you today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What was the trickiest part, and how did you work through it?
  • What are you most proud of?
  • When did you realise "now it's working"?

2. What did you learn today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What can you do now that you couldn't this morning?
  • If you had to teach one thing from today to a friend or sibling, what would it be?
  • Why does that matter — what is it useful for?

3. Where else could you use this?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • Does any school subject use similar thinking (e.g. maths, art, PE)? Which one, and how?

Career path: who works with magnets? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed exploring invisible forces today, you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Physicists — study the fundamental forces of nature, including magnetism and electricity.
  • Electrical Engineers — design motors, generators, and devices that rely on magnetic fields.
  • Geophysicists — study Earth's magnetic field and how it changes over time.
  • Instrument Makers — design and build precision tools like compasses and navigation equipment.
  • Motor & Energy Engineers — build motors and generators that power everything from toys to electric cars.
