Digital Effects

Bring your own drawing to life with AI, then direct your own action movie using augmented reality.

digital media AI & AR filmmaking

Lesson Overview

This lesson explores two different ways technology can add movement and drama to something students make themselves: bringing a hand-drawn character to life with AI-powered animation, and directing a mini action movie using augmented-reality effects on a tablet.

This lesson has two tasks:

  1. Animated Drawings
  2. Action Movie

Theory: How does a still picture or a real room come alive on screen?

Both of today's tools rely on a computer understanding what it's looking at, in real time.

The animation tool works by using AI (artificial intelligence) that has been fine-tuned on a dataset of nearly 180,000 annotated amateur drawings, collected from millions of people who tried Meta's original demo and volunteered their sketches for research. It learns to recognise a head, arms, and legs no matter how wobbly the lines are, then works out where the character's "joints" should be — just like the joints in your own body. Once it knows where those joints are, it can bend and move the drawing at each one, the same way a puppet moves at its strings.

The action-movie tool works differently: it uses augmented reality (AR). The tablet's camera constantly studies the real room through its lens — the floor, the walls, the light — and then draws computer-generated effects (explosions, spaceships, monsters) directly on top of that live video, matching the size, angle, and lighting of the real space so it looks like they're actually there.

Different technology, same basic idea: a computer looks closely at something real — a drawing, a room — and adds movement or magic on top of it.

Good to know

  • The AI behind Animated Drawings was fine-tuned using Meta's Amateur Drawings Dataset — nearly 180,000 annotated sketches, gathered after more than 3 million people tried an earlier version of the demo and chose to share their drawings for research.
  • Augmented reality (AR) — the technology behind Action Movie FX — is also used in real film production. Some crews use AR apps or headsets to preview computer-generated characters or objects directly on set, in real time, before those effects are actually finished in post-production.
  • The earliest hand-drawn animated films, like Disney's first short films, were made by drawing every single frame completely by hand — thousands of individual drawings for just a few minutes of movement.

Task 1: Animated Drawings

TASK 1

Bring your drawing to life

Goal: use a free AI tool to turn a hand-drawn character into a moving animation.

One picture says more than a thousand words. So how much more can a picture say once it's been set in motion? :)

In this task, students use a very simple but extremely fun tool that makes ordinary drawings run, dance, and jump — no drawing skills or experience required. The tool is called Animated Drawings, and it's available for free at sketch.metademolab.com.

Feel free to first explore the site together and watch the demo, to see how it works, before students start drawing.
  1. Each student draws a picture of the character she wants to animate.
  2. Everyone photographs their own drawing using one of the kit's iPads.
  3. From that same iPad, go to sketch.metademolab.com and upload the photographed drawing there.
The kit's iPads are shared for this (passcode 0090), so no one needs their own phone — though if a student would rather use her own phone, that's completely fine too.

✓ Checklist, before uploading a photo

  • The character is drawn on a plain white sheet of paper, without lines, wrinkles, or tears.
  • The drawing is photographed in good light, with as little shadow as possible.

From there, the app walks you through 4 simple steps — each one is clearly explained on screen, with a sample image showing what your drawing should look like at that stage.

Step 1/4

Upload a Drawing. Upload a photo of one character, making sure the arms and legs don't overlap the body.

Step 2/4

Finding the Character. The app draws a box around the character it found. Resize the box so it tightly fits around just one character.

Step 3/4

Separating Character. The app separates the character from the background. If any body part isn't highlighted correctly, use the pen and eraser tools to fix it.

Step 4/4

Finding Character Joints. The app places joints on the character. If your character doesn't have arms or legs drawn in, drag the dots to where they should be.

Press Next, and the animation is ready! On the next screen, choose from a range of pre-programmed movements on the left — dancing, funny moves, jumping, and walking.

Try out different movements — they're very funny! Save a favourite as a video clip straight to your phone by pressing Share, then save video — or send it directly to someone by email.

If some students don't want to draw very detailed pictures, that's completely fine — a class can easily get through several drawings and animations in one lesson. If someone wants to draw something but isn't sure how, there are lots of simple step-by-step drawing tutorials online. Try searching something like "how to draw a teletubby easy" and plenty of tutorials will come up.

Afterwards, drawings made for birthdays or other special occasions can be taken to the next level — sent to mum, dad, or grandparents as a fully animated character. :)

Task 2: Action Movie

TASK 2

Direct your own action scene

Total time: 45–50 minutes · classroom + the whole school building

Goal: film a fun video clip (or several) using the Action Movie FX app. Solved in teams of 3–4.

Tools: iPads with the Action Movie FX app installed. The iPad passcode is 0090.

Each team then gets one iPad with the Action Movie app installed.

Filming can happen anywhere the team chooses — indoors or outdoors. Emphasise to students that this is primarily teamwork: the coolest results happen when everyone contributes, and when the video has a little idea or story behind it, rather than just randomly testing effects. Let imaginations run free, and see who turns out to be the next Hollywood action-movie director! :)

If time allows, finished clips can also be stitched together using the iPad's built-in iMovie app (not required). Make sure to watch all the finished films together at the end, or as each one is completed.
  1. Open the Action Movie app on the iPad.
  2. Press GO to enter the app.
  3. Choose a scene from the effects gallery.
  4. Film your target (a teammate) following the on-screen director's notes — in this case, at least 10 seconds — then press Start.
  5. Film the scene.
  6. Watch the result. If it didn't come out well, delete the clip by pressing the trash can icon.
  7. Decide whether to keep the scene or delete it. If it turned out well, press Share Movie.
  8. Press Save Video, choose the quality to save in (Standard works well and saves storage space), and the clip saves to the iPad's Photos app. Think up your next scenario, and make your next video clip! :)
Using the app is simple, and instructors receive a hands-on walkthrough on-site before the lesson. Anyone who's curious can also download the app themselves and try it out at home. :)

Tips for instructors

  • Demo the Animated Drawings workflow before students start drawing, so everyone knows what a "camera-ready" drawing looks like.
  • For Action Movie, stress team first, effects second — encourage a tiny story or theme, rather than just testing random explosions.
  • Encourage or guide the girls to share the camera-holder role within each team, so everyone gets a turn filming and a turn being filmed.
  • Save a few minutes at the end to watch favourite clips together as a group — it's often the best part of the lesson.

Troubleshooting

  • Drawing doesn't upload, or the character isn't found correctly? Check the lighting and make sure the drawing is on plain white paper with minimal shadows — retake the photo if needed.
  • Character's arms or legs look wrong once animated? Go back to the joints step and drag the points into the correct positions.
  • Action Movie effect doesn't "land" properly? Film a flat, well-lit surface, and hold the camera steady while recording.
  • Can't find a saved video? Check the iPad's Photos app — that's where Action Movie FX saves everything.

Reflection

Keep it spoken and group-based — a quick conversation, not a worksheet.

1. What went well for you today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What was the trickiest part, and how did you work through it?
  • What are you most proud of?
  • When did you realise "now it's working"?

2. What did you learn today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What can you do now that you couldn't this morning?
  • If you had to teach one thing from today to a friend or sibling, what would it be?
  • Why does that matter — what is it useful for?

3. Where else could you use this?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • Does any school subject use similar thinking (e.g. maths, art, PE)? Which one, and how?
  • Have you used something like this before — at home or in a game?
  • Who else uses this in real life — what job might that be? (See the Career path section below for ideas.)
Notes for instructors: keep the discussion spoken and group-based rather than written or individual. The follow-up prompts are a fallback, not a script — if the group answers well on the first try, move on rather than working through the whole list.

Career path: who works with digital effects? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed bringing your drawing to life or directing your own scene today, you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Animators — bring characters to life on screen, frame by frame or with the help of AI tools.
  • AI / Computer Vision Engineers — build the systems that let computers recognise shapes, faces, and movement, like the technology behind Animated Drawings.
  • Film Directors — lead a team to turn an idea into a finished video, just like today's Action Movie teams did.
  • AR/VFX Developers — build the augmented-reality tools that blend computer-generated effects with the real world, in apps and on film sets.
  • App Developers — design and build the apps and tools that make creative technology like this possible.
