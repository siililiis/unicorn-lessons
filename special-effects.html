Special Effects

Smoke, light, and slow motion — turn simple tools into convincing illusions.

#Audio-visual #Engineering #Multimedia

Lesson Overview

This lesson explores how visual special effects are created using technological tools. Students explore the principles of special effects through creative, visually exciting activities, and see how simple tools can be used to create convincing illusions.

This lesson has two tasks:

  1. Bonfire
  2. Water Balloon Explosion

Theory: What are special effects?

Special effects are visual or audio tricks used to create the illusion, on stage or on film, of something that either can't really be shown, or isn't safe to show for real — things like smoke, explosions, rain, or fire. When something would be too dangerous to do for real (like an actual bonfire or an explosion), safe substitutes are used instead: smoke machines, spotlights, models, or tricks of light and shadow.

Stage lighting often uses red, green, and blue light (RGB), which can be combined to create almost any colour tone. This makes it possible, for example, to light plain smoke so that it convincingly resembles real fire.

Good to know

  • Hollywood films often create rain using plain water, sprayed from special shower-head rigs.
  • Fire scenes often use gas flames, or LED lights combined with a smoke machine, instead of real fire.
  • On the set of Titanic, a special pool was used, where the water level and temperature were precisely controlled to keep things safe, while still looking like a real ocean.

Task 1: Bonfire

TASK 1

Build a fake bonfire

Watch the video tutorial

*Add the YouTube link for this tutorial here.

Goal: create the illusion of a bonfire by combining smoke and coloured light.

Short description: students build a "bonfire" together as a group, using a humidifier and LED spotlights. A tube with small holes, connected to a hose, releases mist; the spotlights tint it in fire-like colours, and combined with real logs, this creates a convincing bonfire illusion.

  1. Students work together as one group.
  2. Fill the humidifier with water.
  3. Connect the hose to the humidifier.
  4. Fit the holed tube onto the end of the hose (like a pan flute), so mist escapes through the small holes.
  5. Place the LED spotlights on either side of the tube, plug them in, and choose fire colours (red, orange, yellow).
  6. Disguise the setup: arrange real logs around or in front of the tube the way a real bonfire would look. Cover the wires and any other parts you don't want visible with black fabric.
  7. Darken the room and watch how the smoke and light combine to create the illusion of a fire.
  8. Change the spotlight colours and experiment with different colour combinations.
Theory: special effects rely on the viewer's perception. When light, smoke, or sound are combined correctly, the audience believes what they're seeing is real. That's exactly what an illusion is — the brain interprets cues through familiar past experience. When we see orange light-lit smoke between logs, we automatically associate it with a bonfire. Smoke or mist makes beams of light visible, scattering the rays and giving them "body." That's why light looks more intense and vivid inside smoke than it does simply passing through open air.

*Materials needed: a humidifier, a hose, a tube, spotlights, logs (not included).

⚡ Important info!

The video shows that the spotlights come with a set of preset colours — you should definitely find a shade that works for a bonfire among them. If you'd like to get a very specific colour out of the spotlight, you can also enter the numbers shown on the RGB slider directly into the spotlight. See how to enter RGB colour codes into the spotlight here: add the RGB code tutorial link here

Keep in mind that the humidifier itself is fairly light, which means it can tip over while you're arranging the hose and tube. If the humidifier's body doesn't want to stay put on its base, feel free to tape it down for the duration of the bonfire activity. That said, there are usually plenty of kids around making the bonfire, so keeping the humidifier steady can even become someone's dedicated job. :)

Task 2: Water Balloon Explosion

TASK 2

Capture an explosion in slow motion

Watch the video tutorial

Goal: film a slow-motion video of a water-filled balloon bursting, to see details the naked eye can't catch.

Short description: students fill a balloon with water, fix it in place or hold it hanging, and film it bursting in slow motion using an iPad. The experiment shows how a camera can capture a very fast process, revealing water droplets and the moment of the burst in detail.

  1. Choose a spot where spilled water won't be a problem. Ideally, do this experiment outside. If that's not possible, empty out the kit box and do the experiment over the empty box.
  2. Fill a balloon with water, tie a knot at the end, and if you like, tie a string to the balloon too.
  3. Hold the iPad steady, open the camera app, and select slow-motion mode. Film the balloon bursting. If you have access to a phone with a better camera than the kit's iPad, feel free to use that for filming instead — the better the camera, the higher-quality the result.
  4. Pop the balloon (for example, with a sewing needle).
  5. Watch the recorded video back together and discuss what you saw in slow motion.
Theory: the human eye perceives motion as smooth from a certain speed onward, but we can't consciously make out very fast processes in detail. Slow-motion filming records far more frames per second than a normal video. This lets you play the recording back more slowly afterwards, so that every moment of a fast movement becomes visible. With a good enough camera, filming a water-filled balloon bursting in slow motion lets you see how the balloon's surface splits apart, and how the water briefly holds its shape before the splashes fly apart.

*Materials needed: an iPad, water, balloons, a sewing needle.

Tips for instructors

  • Share the jobs around for the bonfire — steadying the humidifier, arranging logs, and picking spotlight colours can all be different students' roles.
  • Try a few RGB colour combinations before settling on one — small changes make a surprisingly big difference to how convincing the "fire" looks.
  • Do a practice pop yourself first for the water balloon, so students know what to expect before it's their turn.
  • Keep a towel handy near the water balloon station — however careful you are, a little splash is inevitable.

Troubleshooting

  • Bonfire doesn't look convincing? Darken the room further, and adjust the spotlight colours — even small changes make a big difference.
  • Humidifier keeps tipping over? Tape the base down, or assign a student to hold it steady.
  • Slow-motion video looks blurry or too fast to see details? Double-check the camera is actually set to slow-motion mode, not just normal video, and use the best camera available.
  • Balloon won't pop cleanly? Make sure it's filled enough that the skin is under some tension, and use a sharp point rather than a dull one.

Reflection

Keep it spoken and group-based — a quick conversation, not a worksheet.

1. What went well for you today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What was the trickiest part, and how did you work through it?
  • What are you most proud of?
  • When did you realise "now it's working"?

2. What did you learn today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What can you do now that you couldn't this morning?
  • If you had to teach one thing from today to a friend or sibling, what would it be?
  • Why does that matter — what is it useful for?

3. Where else could you use this?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • Does any school subject use similar thinking (e.g. maths, art, PE)? Which one, and how?
  • Have you used something like this before — at home or in a game?
  • Who else uses this in real life — what job might that be? (See the Career path section below for ideas.)
Notes for instructors: keep the discussion spoken and group-based rather than written or individual. The follow-up prompts are a fallback, not a script — if the group answers well on the first try, move on rather than working through the whole list.

Career path: who works with special effects? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed building illusions today, you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Special Effects (SFX) Artists — build the physical effects used on set: smoke, fire, rain, and more.
  • Lighting Designers — plan how light and colour are used to set the mood on stage or on screen.
  • VFX Technicians — combine practical and digital effects to create convincing illusions in film.
  • Set Designers — build the physical environments that make a scene believable.
  • Camera Operators — choose the right techniques, like slow motion, to capture exactly the shot a story needs.
