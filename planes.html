UNICORN SQUAD · LESSON GUIDE

Planes

Fold it, launch it, slow it down — three experiments in how air carries things, pushes them along and holds them back.

physics aerodynamics engineering

Lesson Overview

In this lesson students investigate how air can be used to help movement along, or to slow it down. Through hands-on experiments they fold and fly paper planes, build a parachute that slows a fall, and — if there's time — build a simple "launcher" that gives a plane an extra push and sends it further. The lesson gives students a feel for how the basics of aerodynamics work, and how shape, surface area and air resistance affect the way objects move through the air.

This lesson has three tasks:

  1. Paper planes
  2. The world's simplest launcher
  3. Parachute

Theory: air helps, and air holds back

Air affects every object that moves through it. Sometimes air can be used to help movement along (a plane or a sailing boat, say), and sometimes to slow it down (a parachute). Several forces act on a moving object, and the most important ones are:

  • Gravity — which pulls every object towards the ground.
  • Thrust — which sets the object moving forward.
  • Drag (air resistance) — which slows the movement down.

Task 1: Paper Planes

TASK 1

Fold, fly, and improve

Goal: learn to fold several different paper plane models, and test how the shape of the plane and the position of its wings affect its flight.

Short description: students learn to fold and fly different paper planes and test how the accuracy of the folding, the shape of the plane and the position of the wings affect the length and stability of the flight. After the first test flights they improve their planes with extra cuts or folds, and compare which model flies best.

Activity guide

  • Give every student A4 paper. We always pack paper in the box, but feel free to use old test papers, misprinted sheets or any other spare documents too. It makes no difference to a paper plane what is printed or drawn on it.
  • Fold several different paper planes. As the instructor you can demonstrate the folding yourself, or if you prefer you can put the video up on a big screen and have the students fold along step by step. This video works well to fold along with (three planes in one video): youtu.be/CNEyfJ3mqk4
  • Pay attention to the creases — they need to be accurate and neatly smoothed down, because the quality of the folding affects how the plane flies.
  • First test flights — try flying the planes and watch how they behave in the air (do they fly straight, curve up or down, turn to one side, and so on).
  • Improving — run experiments to make the plane better:
    • add cuts,
    • make extra folds,
    • change the angle of the wings.
    Discuss how each of these changes alters the way the plane flies.
  • Discuss which model turned out to be the best, and why. Which changes improved the flight?
  • If you like, you can also watch a video about a professional paper plane designer, whose planes can fly very long distances, come back like a boomerang, or glide like a bat: youtu.be/3BNg4fDJC8A
Theory — aerodynamics: when we talk about flying, or about moving through the air, we have to talk about aerodynamics — the study of how air moves around objects. It's the laws of aerodynamics that explain how flight is possible at all.
One way to stay up in the air is to be lighter than air — a hot air balloon rises because warm air is lighter than cold air. Planes use a different principle: the special shape of their wings creates a difference in air pressure above and below the wing. A wing is curved on top and flatter underneath, at a slight angle. That means the air has to travel a longer path over the top of the wing, and it does so faster, while the air underneath moves more slowly. Faster-moving air creates lower pressure and slower air creates higher pressure — and the difference between them is lift, which is what holds a plane up. For all of this to work, the plane has to be moving forward fast enough. The same principle of curvature and pressure difference is used elsewhere too — in helicopter rotor blades, in kites, and in the sails of sailing boats, which work just like a wing.
The same aerodynamic principles that govern real aircraft are at play in the flight of a paper plane. Four forces act on the plane:
  • Lift — created by the movement of air around the wing.
  • Gravity — the pull of the Earth, which draws the plane downwards.
  • Drag — air resistance, which slows the movement down.
  • Thrust — whatever sets the plane moving (a throw from your hand, for example).
So a paper plane's flight depends very much on its shape, the position of its wings, and the way it is thrown.

Good to know

The world record for a paper plane flight is over 88 metres (set in the USA in 2012). There are professional paper plane designers who create models that fly like a boomerang, glide like a bat, or stay in the air for a very long time — and YouTube holds hundreds of tutorials for folding all kinds of paper planes.

Task 2: The World's Simplest Launcher

TASK 2

Give the plane an extra push

Goal: test how elastic force can give an object extra speed, and use it to increase the plane's flight distance.

Short description: students make a simple add-on for their paper planes out of a rubber band — a "launcher" — that lets the planes fly further than throwing them by hand ever could.

*The simple launcher appears in the video tutorial from 2:20.

Activity guide

  1. Fold yourself a paper plane (you can use the one you made in the previous task).
  2. Punch a hole in the bottom edge of the plane, at the point where you normally hold it in your hand.
  3. Push a rubber band through the hole and fasten it to the plane.
  4. Hook the rubber band over your finger or thumb, stretch it back and release it cleanly — the plane gets an extra push and flies further.
Good to know: the very same principle — the use of elastic force — has been used throughout history in catapults, slingshots and crossbows. When you stretch a rubber band it stores potential energy, which turns into kinetic energy when you let go and sets the plane moving. Springs in clocks and trampolines store potential energy in the same way, turning it into movement at the moment of the jump.
Instructor note: there are a great many different ways to build a paper plane launcher. For inspiration, search the internet for paper plane launcher.

Task 3: Parachute

TASK 3

Slow the fall down

Goal: build a simple parachute that slows the fall of a small object, and experience how air resistance affects the speed of movement.

Short description: students make a parachute for a small figure, test how it works, and compare which versions slow the fall best. The parachutes are dropped from a higher spot to the floor below, and the class watches how the size and the material of the canopy affect the fall.

*The parachute task appears in the video tutorial from 11:07.

Activity guide

  1. Each student makes a parachute for their own figure (a Lego person or a table tennis ball, for example).
  2. The parachute can be made from a paper napkin or from a plastic bag. Attach strings or short lengths of yarn to the corners of the canopy, and fasten those to the figure.
  3. For testing, find a higher spot (a staircase, a balcony, a first-floor window or similar).
  4. Drop the figure with its parachute and watch how it falls compared with a fall without a canopy.
Theory: a parachute works thanks to air resistance. When a body moves quickly through the air, the air begins to push back against it. A parachute increases the surface area of the object and therefore creates greater air resistance, which in turn slows the fall.

Good to know

Without a parachute a person falls at around 200 km/h; with one, at only about 20 km/h. Parachutes aren't only for skydivers — they're also used to slow down spacecraft and racing cars, and in athletes' training, where a small chute adds resistance to running and makes the session harder. A parachute "backpack" actually contains three canopies: the main one, the reserve, and a small pilot chute that pulls the others open. Today's parachutes are steerable and safe, but early military parachutes didn't even let the jumper choose where to land.

Tips for instructors

  • Creases decide everything. Demonstrate one fold and have everyone press it flat with a fingernail — accurate folding matters far more than which model they choose.
  • Agree a flight lane and a "one thrower at a time" rule before the first plane goes up, especially for the launcher task.
  • Change one thing at a time when improving a plane — otherwise nobody can tell which change actually helped.
  • Keep a stack of scrap paper handy — old worksheets and misprints work perfectly, and students will want several attempts.
  • Check the drop spot for the parachutes in advance and agree who stands below.

Troubleshooting

  • Plane dives straight into the floor? Bend the back edges of the wings up very slightly, and throw more gently and level.
  • Plane shoots up, stalls and drops? The wings are bent up too much — flatten them, and check that both wings match.
  • Plane always veers to one side? The two halves aren't symmetrical — refold carefully along the centre line.
  • The rubber band tears the nose? Punch the hole a little further back from the edge, and reinforce it with a piece of tape.
  • Parachute falls as fast as the bare figure? The canopy is too small or the strings are tangled — make it bigger and check all the strings are the same length.

Reflection

Keep it spoken and group-based — a quick conversation, not a worksheet.

1. What went well for you today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What was the trickiest part, and how did you work through it?
  • What are you most proud of?
  • When did you realise "now it's working"?

2. What did you learn today?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • What can you do now that you couldn't this morning?
  • If you had to teach one thing from today to a friend or sibling, what would it be?
  • Why does that matter — what is it useful for?

3. Where else could you use this?

If the group gets stuck, try
  • Does any school subject use similar thinking (e.g. maths, art, PE)? Which one, and how?
  • Have you used something like this before — at home or in a game?
  • Who else uses this in real life — what job might that be? (See the Career path section below for ideas.)
Notes for instructors: keep the discussion spoken and group-based rather than written or individual. The follow-up prompts are a fallback, not a script — if the group answers well on the first try, move on rather than working through the whole list.

Career path: who works with flight? For instructors — optional

If you enjoyed making things fly — and making them fall slowly — you're touching on a field full of real careers. For example:

  • Aerospace Engineers — design aircraft, rockets and spacecraft, and everything that keeps them in the air.
  • Aerodynamicists — study how air flows around wings, cars and buildings, and shape them to work with it.
  • Pilots — fly aircraft, using the four forces every single flight.
  • Parachute & Safety Equipment Designers — design canopies, harnesses and airbags that slow things down safely.
  • Prototype Makers — build and test physical models, exactly like today's fold-fly-improve loop, before anything big gets built.
